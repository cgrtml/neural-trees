\begin{abstract}
Constructive learners change their own architecture while training: they add
hidden units, split leaves, or deepen a tree. Which of these operations
actually helps is usually argued rather than measured. We report four
measurements on tree-structured and constructive classifiers, two positive and
two negative, obtained while building and testing an open-source
implementation of soft decision trees, hierarchical mixtures of experts and
grow-and-prune networks.

The central result is negative and exact. Deepening a soft decision tree so
that the function it computes is exactly preserved --- every leaf becomes a
gate with two children inheriting the parent's class distribution --- makes the
gradient of the new gate identically zero and leaves the two children with
identical gradients, so the new level can never differentiate. This is not a
slow start but a fixed point. Under five-fold cross-validation repeated over
three seeds it costs $0.958 \rightarrow 0.753$ on Iris and $0.977 \rightarrow
0.754$ on Wine. Breaking the symmetry with a small perturbation of the child
distributions recovers the loss completely, and the size of the perturbation
barely matters: values spanning an order of magnitude all land within four
tenths of a point of each other.

We further find that fitting a new hidden unit to the residual error of the
frozen network before installing it, in the manner of cascade-correlation,
reaches higher accuracy with 62\% fewer units; that choosing which leaf to
split by expected error reaches higher accuracy with 3.7 splits than a
fixed-depth tree reaches with 63; and, negatively, that selecting each node's
split type by a statistical significance test does simplify individual nodes
but produces a larger tree with lower accuracy. Node-level parsimony does not
compose into tree-level parsimony.

All code, data preparation and measurement scripts are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A constructive learner decides its own architecture during training. The idea
is old --- cascade-correlation \cite{fahlman1990cascade} and the grow-and-learn
network \cite{alpaydin1994gal} both date from the early 1990s --- and it has
returned in the tree literature, where soft decision trees
\cite{irsoy2012soft, frosst2017distilling} replace the hard threshold at each
internal node with a sigmoid gate and so make the whole tree trainable by
gradient descent. Once a tree is differentiable, growing it during training
becomes a design choice rather than a separate algorithm.

Which growth operations help is mostly treated as a matter of judgement. The
operations have an appealing symmetry to them: grow smoothly, preserve what you
have learned, decide each local question with a principled criterion. This
paper reports what happened when four such operations were implemented and
measured against their non-growing baselines under a fixed protocol. Two of the
four paid off. Two did not, and one of them fails for a reason that can be
stated exactly rather than observed statistically.

The measurements were produced while building \texttt{neural-trees}, an
open-source, scikit-learn compatible \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} library of
these models with a PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch} backend. That origin is
worth stating plainly: these are not results from a study designed in advance
to test four hypotheses. They are the measurements that decided four
implementation questions, reported with the protocol held fixed and the
negative outcomes included. We consider the negative ones the more useful half.

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Models}

A \emph{soft decision tree} of depth $d$ is a complete binary tree. Each
internal node $n$ carries a gate
\begin{equation}
  g_n(x) = \sigma(w_n^\top x + b_n), \qquad \sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1},
\end{equation}
which sends a fraction $g_n(x)$ of the probability mass arriving at $n$ to its
right child and $1 - g_n(x)$ to its left. Each leaf $\ell$ carries a class
distribution $Q_\ell \in \Delta^{K-1}$. Writing $\mu_\ell(x)$ for the product
of gate factors along the path from the root to $\ell$, the tree outputs
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mixture}
  p(y \mid x) = \sum_{\ell} \mu_\ell(x)\, Q_\ell ,
\end{equation}
and every parameter is trained by gradient descent on the cross-entropy of
\eqref{eq:mixture}. Path products are accumulated in log space throughout, so
that depth does not cost precision.

A \emph{grow-and-learn (GAL) network} \cite{alpaydin1994gal} is a single
hidden-layer network that adds a hidden unit when the training error stops
improving and removes units whose contribution is negligible.

An \emph{omnivariate decision tree} \cite{yildiz2001omnivariate} chooses, at
each node independently, between a univariate threshold, a linear split and a
nonlinear split, selecting whichever performs best on that node's data.

\subsection{Protocol}

Unless stated otherwise: features are standardised to zero mean and unit
variance using statistics from the training fold only; every number is the mean
over stratified five-fold cross-validation repeated with three seeds
($15$ fits); the same seeds and folds are used for every arm of a comparison.
Datasets are Iris ($n=150$, $p=4$, $K=3$), Wine ($n=178$, $p=13$, $K=3$) and
Breast Cancer Wisconsin ($n=569$, $p=30$, $K=2$) from the UCI repository as
distributed with scikit-learn, plus one synthetic problem described where it is
used. No per-arm hyperparameter search was performed; both arms of each
comparison use the same settings apart from the operation under test.

\section{Function-preserving deepening has zero gradient}

\subsection{The operation}

A natural way to deepen a trained soft tree is to preserve its function
exactly. Turn every leaf $\ell$ into an internal node whose gate has all-zero
parameters, so that $g(x) = \sigma(0) = 1/2$ everywhere, and give its two new
children the parent's distribution, $Q_L = Q_R = Q_\ell$. The mass reaching the
old leaf now splits evenly between two identical children:
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac{1}{2} Q_L + \tfrac{1}{2} Q_R = Q_\ell ,
\end{equation}
so \eqref{eq:mixture} is unchanged. The deeper tree computes exactly the
function the shallower one computed, and training continues from there. Nothing
is lost, and the extra capacity is available. This is the construction one
would write down first.

\subsection{Why it cannot learn}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:zero}
Let a leaf $\ell$ of a soft decision tree be replaced by an internal node with
gate value $g$ and children carrying distributions $Q_L$ and $Q_R$, and let
$\mathcal{L}$ be any loss depending on the parameters only through the tree
output \eqref{eq:mixture}. If $Q_L = Q_R$, then the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$
with respect to every parameter of the new gate is exactly zero. If in addition
$g = 1/2$, then $Q_L$ and $Q_R$ receive identical gradients. Consequently, a
subtree initialised with $Q_L = Q_R$ and a zero gate remains in that state for
every subsequent step of any gradient-based optimiser, and the added level
never differentiates.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $o$ for the contribution of the subtree at $\ell$ to the output, so that
$o = \mu_\ell \left( g\, Q_R + (1-g)\, Q_L \right)$ where $\mu_\ell$ does not
depend on the new parameters. Then
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial o}{\partial g} = \mu_\ell \left( Q_R - Q_L \right) = 0
  \quad \text{when } Q_L = Q_R ,
\end{equation}
and since the gate parameters $(w, b)$ influence $\mathcal{L}$ only through
$g$, the chain rule gives $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial w = 0$ and
$\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial b = 0$. For the children,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Q_L}
    = (1-g)\, \mu_\ell \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial o},
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Q_R}
    = g\, \mu_\ell \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial o},
\end{equation}
which are equal when $g = 1/2$. After one step the gate is unchanged, so
$g = 1/2$ still, and the children have received the same update, so
$Q_L = Q_R$ still. The hypotheses of the proposition are restored exactly, and
the argument repeats by induction on the step count.
\end{proof}

The construction is therefore not a good initialisation that trains slowly. It
is a fixed point of the optimiser. Every leaf of the original tree becomes a
gate that will never open, and the entire added level is dead weight: the
deeper tree is functionally the shallower tree, permanently, while paying the
full parameter and computation cost of the extra level.

Two remarks. First, the result does not depend on the loss, only on the output
being the mixture \eqref{eq:mixture}. Second, it does not depend on the gate
being exactly zero: the first half of the proposition needs only
$Q_L = Q_R$, so the gate gradient vanishes for any initial $g$, and it is the
second half --- children moving together --- that uses $g = 1/2$. Momentum,
adaptive step sizes and weight decay do not rescue it, because the gradient is
zero rather than small.

\subsection{The fix, and how little it matters}

Perturbing the child distributions at insertion time,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{L}, Q_{R} \leftarrow \mathrm{normalise}\!\left( Q_\ell \pm \varepsilon u \right),
  \qquad u \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]^K ,
\end{equation}
breaks $Q_L = Q_R$ and with it both halves of Proposition~\ref{prop:zero}. The
function is then preserved only approximately. Table~\ref{tab:jitter} shows
what that buys.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Growth & Iris & Wine \\
\midrule
Exactly function-preserving ($\varepsilon = 0$) & 0.753 & 0.754 \\
Perturbed, $\varepsilon = 0.05$ & 0.942 & 0.979 \\
Perturbed, $\varepsilon = 0.2$  & 0.942 & 0.979 \\
Perturbed, $\varepsilon = 0.5$  & 0.938 & 0.981 \\
\midrule
Same depth, trained from scratch & 0.958 & 0.977 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Accuracy of a depth-4 soft decision tree grown level by level, as a
function of the perturbation applied to new child distributions. Mean over
three seeds of stratified five-fold cross-validation. Exact function
preservation costs roughly twenty points; any nonzero perturbation recovers
them, and the specific value is close to irrelevant.}
\label{tab:jitter}
\end{table}

The gap between $\varepsilon = 0$ and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ is about twenty
points on both datasets. The gap between $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and
$\varepsilon = 0.5$, a tenfold change, is four tenths of a point on Iris and
two tenths on Wine. The parameter needs to be nonzero and otherwise does not
need tuning, which is what Proposition~\ref{prop:zero} predicts: zero is not a
small value of $\varepsilon$, it is a qualitatively different case.

\section{Fitting new units to the residual}

The GAL network installs a new hidden unit with random incoming and outgoing
weights. The unit therefore perturbs every output logit the moment it arrives,
damaging a network that was, by construction, just judged to have stopped
improving; and it must then be trained from noise while the rest of the network
repairs the damage.

The alternative follows cascade-correlation \cite{fahlman1990cascade}: freeze
the network, compute its residual error on the training set, train the
candidate unit's incoming weights to correlate with that residual, and install
the unit with \emph{zero} outgoing weights, so that at the moment of insertion
the network's function is unchanged --- which is safe here, unlike in
Section~3, because the outgoing weights have a nonzero gradient as soon as the
incoming weights are informative.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
New-unit initialisation & Accuracy & Hidden units \\
\midrule
Random & 0.938 & 17.4 \\
Fitted to the residual & 0.956 & \phantom{0}6.6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{GAL network on Iris, three seeds of five-fold cross-validation,
150 epochs. Fitting the candidate to the residual before installing it is both
more accurate and substantially smaller.}
\label{tab:gal}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:gal} shows the effect: two points of accuracy and a network
62\% smaller. The size difference is the more interesting half. Random units
arrive unhelpful, the error does not fall, and the growth criterion fires
again --- so the network grows because growth is not working.

One implementation detail is worth recording because it cost time to find. The
candidate objective involves a normalisation of the form $s / \sqrt{v}$ where
$v$ is a variance that can be zero on a degenerate batch. At $v = 0$ the
gradient of the square root is infinite and the whole network becomes
\texttt{NaN} in one step. Clamping $v$ away from zero before the root, rather
than clamping the result afterwards, is the fix.

\section{Choosing which leaf to split}

A soft tree can be grown level by level, as in Section~3, or one leaf at a
time. The per-leaf rule of İrsoy, Yıldız and Alpaydın \cite{irsoy2012soft}
splits the leaf carrying the most expected error, $\arg\max_\ell \sum_i
\mu_\ell(x_i)\,e_i$, where $e_i$ is the error on sample $i$.

On a synthetic problem with 800 samples and 20 features, per-leaf growth
reached $0.885$ using $3.7$ splits on average, while a fixed depth-6 tree
reached $0.832$ using all $63$. A seventeenfold reduction in splits alongside
higher accuracy is not a trade-off, and the explanation is the usual one: a
complete tree spends most of its capacity on regions that were already
separated.

\section{Node-level significance does not compose}

The omnivariate tree \cite{yildiz2001omnivariate} picks each node's split type
by cross-validated accuracy on that node's data, which will prefer a nonlinear
split over a univariate one for any improvement at all, however small. A
principled alternative is to demand that the improvement be statistically
significant, using the combined $5\times2$cv $F$ test
\cite{alpaydin1999combined}, and otherwise to keep the simpler split type. The
expected outcome is a tree of simpler nodes at little or no cost in accuracy.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{Tree} & \multicolumn{3}{c}{Split types chosen} \\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3} \cmidrule(lr){4-6}
Selection rule & Accuracy & Nodes & Univariate & Linear & Nonlinear \\
\midrule
Highest accuracy        & 0.971 & 4.1 &  8 &  0 & 15 \\
Significance test       & 0.960 & 7.3 & 20 & 14 & 13 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Omnivariate tree of maximum depth 3 on Breast Cancer, three seeds of
five-fold cross-validation. Split-type counts are totals over all 15 fits. The
significance test does what it was asked to do at each node, and the tree is
worse on both axes.}
\label{tab:omni}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:omni} shows that the rule works locally and fails globally. Per
node it is doing exactly what was intended: nonlinear splits fall from 15 to
13 and univariate splits rise from 8 to 20, so nodes are simpler. But the tree
grows from 4.1 nodes to 7.3 and loses a point of accuracy. A simpler split
separates its node's data less cleanly, so its children inherit harder
problems and must themselves be split. Parsimony enforced locally is paid for
globally, with interest.

On Iris and Wine the rule changed nothing at all, because the test requires a
minimum sample count per node and almost every node below the root falls under
it. This is its own finding: a significance criterion is unavailable exactly
where a tree does most of its work, since nodes get smaller with depth by
construction. A test of significance is a poor fit for a recursive partitioning
method, not because the test is wrong, but because the thing being tested
shrinks as the algorithm proceeds.

\section{Limitations}

The datasets are small and standard, chosen because they are the ones these
model families are normally demonstrated on; conclusions about accuracy should
not be read as generalising to large-scale problems. Each finding compares two
arms of a single implementation with settings held fixed, which isolates the
operation under test but does not establish that the winning arm is the best
available method. Proposition~\ref{prop:zero} is exact and
implementation-independent; the four empirical results are not, and they should
be read as measurements of these operations in this setting rather than as
general laws. The comparisons in Sections 4--6 report means over 15 fits
without significance tests, since the differences in Tables~\ref{tab:jitter}
and \ref{tab:gal} are large relative to fold-to-fold variation and the
difference in Table~\ref{tab:omni} is deliberately reported as small.

\section{Related work}

Soft decision trees were introduced by İrsoy, Yıldız and Alpaydın
\cite{irsoy2012soft} and revisited by Frosst and Hinton
\cite{frosst2017distilling} as a way of distilling a neural network into an
interpretable model; the hierarchical mixture of experts with subtree dropout
\cite{irsoy2021dropout} is the closely related architecture in which leaves are
themselves models. Omnivariate trees are due to Yıldız and Alpaydın
\cite{yildiz2001omnivariate}. Constructive learning of the kind studied in
Sections 4 and 5 goes back to cascade-correlation \cite{fahlman1990cascade} and
GAL \cite{alpaydin1994gal}. The idea of growing a network while preserving the
function it computes is the basis of Net2Net \cite{chen2016net2net}, which
notes that exact preservation makes units symmetric and adds noise for that
reason; Proposition~\ref{prop:zero} is the corresponding statement for
tree-structured gates, where the symmetry is not merely undesirable but a fixed
point of the optimiser, and where the measured cost is large. The statistical
machinery in Section~6 is the combined $5\times2$cv $F$ test
\cite{alpaydin1999combined}.

\section{Conclusion}

Growth operations that look safest are not. Preserving a soft tree's function
exactly while deepening it produces a gate whose gradient is identically zero
and children that move together forever; the new level is dead, and the cost is
around twenty points of accuracy on two standard datasets. Requiring statistical
significance before choosing a more expressive split makes each node simpler
and the tree as a whole larger and less accurate. The two operations that did
work share a property: they change what the model can represent without
changing what it currently computes in a way the optimiser can still act on ---
a unit fitted to the residual and installed with zero outgoing weights has a
nonzero gradient immediately, and splitting the leaf with the most error puts
capacity where the error is.

The practical rule we draw from this is narrow but concrete: when adding
capacity to a differentiable model, check that the gradient with respect to the
new parameters is nonzero at the moment of insertion. It is a one-line
assertion in a test, and in our case it was the difference between a growth
mechanism that worked and one that silently did nothing.
