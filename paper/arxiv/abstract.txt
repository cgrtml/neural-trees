A classifier that grows during training has to decide where its new capacity goes. I measured four such decisions in tree-structured and constructive models: how to deepen a soft decision tree, how to initialise a new hidden unit, which leaf to split next, and which model class to give each node. All four are usually presented as free improvements. None of them is. 

One of them cannot learn at all, and I prove why. Suppose a soft decision tree is deepened without changing the function it computes: every leaf becomes a gate, and its two children inherit the parent's class distribution. Then the gradient with respect to the new gate is identically zero. If the gate starts at 1/2, the two children also receive identical gradients, so they remain identical and the gate's gradient remains zero. The state reproduces itself at every step. This degeneracy is stronger than the symmetry that Net2Net breaks with noise, and stronger than the saddle point that splitting steepest descent escapes with second-order information. Exact preservation costs 19.6 accuracy points on Iris, 19.1 on Wine and 55.6 on Digits relative to a tree of the same depth trained from scratch, and it inflates the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Perturbing the child distributions removes the problem, and the size of the perturbation barely matters. 

The other three decisions are trade-offs. Fitting a new hidden unit to the residual error before installing it yields a smaller network on all three datasets but not a more accurate one; on Digits it is significantly worse. Splitting the leaf with the most expected error reached an accuracy of 0.885 using 3.7 splits where a complete depth-six tree needed 63, yet lost 4.3 points on a harder problem. Requiring statistical significance before a node is given a more expressive split made the tree larger and less accurate.
