Constructive learners change their own architecture while training: they add hidden units, split leaves, or deepen a tree. Which of these operations actually helps is usually argued rather than measured. We report four measurements on tree-structured and constructive classifiers, two positive and two negative, obtained while building and testing an open-source implementation of soft decision trees, hierarchical mixtures of experts and grow-and-prune networks. 

The central result is negative and exact. Deepening a soft decision tree so that the function it computes is exactly preserved -- every leaf becomes a gate with two children inheriting the parent's class distribution -- makes the gradient of the new gate identically zero and leaves the two children with identical gradients, so the new level can never differentiate. This is not a slow start but a fixed point. Under five-fold cross-validation repeated over three seeds it costs 0.958 -> 0.753 on Iris and 0.977 -> 0.754 on Wine. Breaking the symmetry with a small perturbation of the child distributions recovers the loss completely, and the size of the perturbation barely matters: values spanning an order of magnitude all land within four tenths of a point of each other. 

We further find that fitting a new hidden unit to the residual error of the frozen network before installing it, in the manner of cascade-correlation, reaches higher accuracy with 62% fewer units; that choosing which leaf to split by expected error reaches higher accuracy with 3.7 splits than a fixed-depth tree reaches with 63; and, negatively, that selecting each node's split type by a statistical significance test does simplify individual nodes but produces a larger tree with lower accuracy. Node-level parsimony does not compose into tree-level parsimony. 

All code, data preparation and measurement scripts are public.
