Constructive classifiers add structure while they train: a level to a tree, a unit to a hidden layer, a split at a leaf. I measured four such growth decisions in tree-structured and constructive models under one fixed protocol. None of them is the free improvement it is usually taken to be. 

The main result is a failure with an exact cause. The natural way to deepen a soft decision tree without disturbing it is to turn every leaf into a gate whose two children inherit the parent's class distribution. I prove that this leaves the gradient of every new gate identically zero, and that with the gate at 1/2 the two children receive identical gradients. The added level therefore never learns: the state reproduces itself at every step of gradient descent. This is a stronger degeneracy than the symmetry that Net2Net breaks with noise, or the saddle point that splitting steepest descent escapes with second-order information. Over three seeds of five-fold cross-validation, the exact construction loses 19.6 accuracy points on Iris, 19.1 on Wine and 55.6 on Digits against a tree of the same depth trained from scratch. A small random perturbation of the children removes the problem, and its size barely matters. 

The other three decisions are trade-offs, not gains. Fitting a new hidden unit to the residual error before installing it gives a smaller network on every dataset but not a more accurate one, and on Digits it is significantly worse. Splitting the leaf with the largest expected error reaches 0.885 accuracy with 3.7 splits where a complete depth-six tree uses 63, but loses 4.3 points on a harder problem. Requiring statistical significance before a node receives a more expressive split makes the tree larger and less accurate. 

Every number in the paper is inserted from the measurement script's output.
